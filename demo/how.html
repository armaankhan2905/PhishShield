How a message is checked

Several independent checks look at the message. A score is built only from what they found, and every point comes with a reason you can read.

  1. Read the message

    Links are pulled out of the text, including ones written without https://. Gmail's redirect wrappers are removed. For saved emails, the sender, reply-to and subject are read, and HTML is turned into plain text without running anything in it. Screenshots are read on this computer: the text with a local OCR model, and any QR code is decoded to see what it really does. Links are only read, never opened.

  2. Check UPI payments

    A UPI QR code, a "collect request" and your UPI PIN can only ever send money. A message that says to scan, approve or enter your PIN to receive a refund, prize or cashback is flagged, and so is a QR code whose payee name claims a bank while the money goes to a personal UPI ID, or a UPI ID like sbi.kyc.support@okaxis.

  3. Check every link's real owner

    Each link's real domain is worked out with the Public Suffix List (so login.sbi.co.in and sbi.co.in.evil.xyz are told apart). It's then compared with 36 commonly impersonated brands for look-alike characters, misspellings, brand-plus-phishing-word names and brands placed in front of someone else's domain.

  4. Check the sender

    Does the display name claim a brand the sending domain doesn't belong to? Is it a free email address posing as a company? Do replies go somewhere else? Does a link's visible text show a different site from where it really goes?

  5. Read the wording

    Rules look for requests (share your OTP, pay a fee, install AnyDesk), threats, prizes and pressure. "Never share your OTP" is a warning, not a request, and isn't flagged.

  6. Ask two small models

    A URL model scores each link from its spelling alone. A text model scores the wording. Both run on this server; their probabilities are calibrated on held-out data.

  7. Score only what was found

    Proven technical evidence counts in full. Patterns count once each and are capped, so repeating "urgent" adds nothing. Phishing needs proven evidence. A model alone can reach suspicious but never phishing. A message with nothing to act on (no link, number, app or payment) stays safe. When an email's sender and all of its links belong to the same organisation and it asks for nothing (no details, payment, login, call or app), the models count for a quarter: they tend to over-flag ordinary newsletters and sign-in emails. A check that didn't run lowers the confidence; it never counts as safety.

  8. Explain it

    A plain summary is built from the reasons. On your own computer, a local AI model (Ollama) can rewrite it in plain words. It sees only the reasons and a copy of the message with personal data removed, and an answer that adds anything else is thrown away.

What the labels mean

Proven
A technical fact anyone can check, such as a link to sbi-kyc.xyz claiming to be SBI.
Pattern
Wording or link traits common in scams but also seen in real messages, such as urgency or a shortened link.
Model
A machine-learning score. Useful, but never enough on its own for a phishing verdict.
